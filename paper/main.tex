\begin{abstract}
Connected vehicles broadcast Basic Safety Messages (BSMs) that state their position, speed and heading several times per second, and every
receiving vehicle builds its picture of the road from them. A vehicle that broadcasts false content (a shifted or frozen position, a wrong speed,
replayed or delayed messages, or a flood of messages) can make its neighbours brake, change lanes or ignore a real hazard. Digital signatures
do not help here: they prove who sent a message, not that its content is true, so the content must be judged on the receiving vehicle itself.
Current learned detectors leave four gaps. They judge each sender on its own, ignoring the other vehicles the receiver hears at the same time; they
are evaluated on few attack types and often report accuracy alone, which hides the false-alarm rate; they label every message of an attacker
vehicle as malicious even while it behaves; and they are rarely tested against attackers that tell a physically consistent story or that collude.
We study the receiver's view. For every receiving vehicle and second, the 16 nearest senders over the last 10 seconds are classified as honest or
misbehaving by a small transformer (144k parameters) that attends over each sender's history and across the neighbouring senders. Its inputs add
plausibility checks to the raw claims: kinematic consistency, a road map learned from honest traffic, exact repeats of earlier positions and
positions copied from another sender's trail. Training adds windows in which honest senders tell careful, mutually consistent lies, and a
receiver-side memory keeps suspicion of senders already flagged. The system is implemented in PyTorch and trains in 10--15 minutes on a laptop GPU.
On the public VeReMi Extension benchmark (simulated, 14 of its 19 attack types, 68,971 test windows, two seeds) it reaches precision 98.16\%,
recall 97.78\%, F1 97.97\% and accuracy 98.74\% when an attacker is scored only in windows where it sends falsified messages, and F1 97.01\% when
every window of an attacker vehicle counts, against F1 94.9\% for the same model without the checks and 78.9\% for the dataset's
speed-consistency rule under that stricter labelling. Neighbour attention adds a small but significant 0.3 F1 points and lowers the false-positive
rate from 0.90\% to 0.60\%, but its benefit disappears when most neighbours are attackers. A collusion stress test shows why the checks matter:
before them, coordinated colluders with consistent lies were caught in at most 4\% of cases; with them, in 70--90\% at a 1\% false-positive rate,
including a spoofed convoy never seen in training. Lies that stay on the road and obey the physics remain hard (23--48\%), which marks the limit of
detection from message content alone.
\end{abstract}

\section{The Problem}
\label{sec:problem}
\textbf{Setting.} In V2X networks every vehicle periodically broadcasts a BSM with its claimed position, speed, heading and acceleration.
Message signatures prove \emph{who} sent a message but not that its \emph{content} is true: an authenticated insider, or a vehicle with a faulty or
manipulated sensor, can send false content that passes all cryptographic checks~\cite{ruj2011, boualouache2022}. Misbehaviour detection must therefore
judge the content itself, on the receiving vehicle, within a fraction of a second, without ground truth about where the other vehicles really are.

\textbf{Task.} Let receiver $r$ at second $t$ hear a set of senders. We take the $K{=}16$ senders closest to $r$ by claimed distance and their
claims over the last $T{=}10$ seconds (messages binned to one-second steps) and output, for each sender, the probability that it misbehaves. A window
is formed every 3 seconds; each sender slot is one decision. The receiver knows its own position but never the true position of a sender.

\textbf{Threat model.} The attacker is an insider: a vehicle with valid credentials that controls the content and the timing of its own
messages. It may lie about its position, speed, heading and acceleration, replay or delay messages, copy other vehicles' content, or flood the
channel. Several attackers may coordinate (collusion). The attacker cannot change what the receiver measures about itself, and it does not
control the honest vehicles. The detector runs on the receiver and sees only received messages and the receiver's own state; it has no access to
roadside units, other receivers' decisions or the physical layer.

\textbf{Why it is hard.} Honest claims are themselves noisy (GPS error of a few metres, sensor error on speed and heading), vehicles stop
and accelerate legitimately, and a receiver hears a changing set of senders whose messages arrive irregularly. The detector must keep false alarms
below about 1\%, because every false alarm discards an honest vehicle's data, and must decide within seconds. A lie that is small, smooth and on
the road can look exactly like an honest vehicle, and when several attackers agree, the neighbours a receiver could compare against are partly
malicious themselves.

\textbf{Attacks.} We use VeReMi Extension~\cite{kamel2020}, the successor of VeReMi~\cite{vanderheijden2018}: simulated traffic of the Luxembourg
scenario with 30\% misbehaving vehicles and 19 attack types. We run 14 of them: constant, random and offset positions (ConstPos, RandomPos,
ConstPosOffset, RandomPosOffset), constant, random and offset speeds (ConstSpeed, RandomSpeed, ConstSpeedOffset), EventualStop (the vehicle claims
to have stopped), Disruptive and DataReplay (content taken from other vehicles), DelayedMessages, and three denial-of-service variants (DoS,
DoSRandom, DoSDisruptive). The four Sybil attacks are not run, and the RandomSpeedOffset archive is excluded because both of its time slices
contain the same data. The data are simulated; no claim is made about real road traffic. Table~\ref{tab:attacks} lists what each attack
falsifies and how many attacker slots it contributes to the test set.

\textbf{What counts as misbehaviour.} The benchmark labels vehicles: every message of an attacker vehicle is ``malicious''. Some attackers,
however, send true messages for part of the time; EventualStop, for instance, drives normally until it fakes its stop. Using the per-message ground
truth shipped with the dataset, we also score at the message level (Section~\ref{sec:labels}) and report both.

\section{Gaps in Current Work}
\label{sec:gaps}
\textbf{G1: one sender at a time.} Most learned detectors classify a sender, or a pair of consecutive messages, in isolation, from location and
movement features~\cite{so2018, singh2019, sharma2022}. Transformer detectors have appeared~\cite{li2025attentionguard, kalogiannis2026pampos},
including PAMPOS, which flags deviations from a predicted trajectory without attack labels~\cite{kalogiannis2026pampos}. What a receiver can gain
from comparing a sender with the senders around it, and what it loses when those neighbours are themselves malicious, is rarely measured.

\textbf{G2: metrics.} Results are often a single accuracy figure~\cite{hakeem2025}. Accuracy hides the false-positive rate, which decides whether a
detector is usable (every false alarm discards an honest vehicle's data), and it says nothing about detection latency.

\textbf{G3: few attacks, unclear comparisons.} Many studies use the original VeReMi with five position attacks~\cite{vanderheijden2018}. The
survey of Abdel Hakeem and Kim~\cite{hakeem2025}, which we take as our reference point, lists 97.37\% for VeReMi with accuracy, precision, recall and
F1 reported together, and per-study accuracies of 95--97\%~\cite{so2018, singh2019}; it does not state splits or label definitions, so such
numbers are hard to compare.

\textbf{G4: consistent lies and collusion.} Plausibility checks~\cite{so2019} catch claims that are impossible, but an attacker can fake a
trajectory that is internally consistent, and several attackers can agree on one story. Reputation schemes assume most neighbours are
honest~\cite{gyawali2020}; learned detectors are rarely tested against coordinated, physically consistent lies.

\textbf{G5: vehicle-level labels.} Scoring every message of an attacker vehicle as malicious counts honest-looking periods as missed detections,
which mixes up the detector's errors with the attacker's choice to behave.

\textbf{G6: where the detector runs.} Recent work moves detection into federated learning, to keep vehicle data
private~\cite{marmol2024, demir2025}, or into large language models at the network edge~\cite{hamhoum2024}; others target Sybil
attacks~\cite{thomas2024, khan2026} or combine an LSTM detector with a protocol that reacts to detections~\cite{franceschini2024}. These directions
need a strong local detector to start from: one that runs on a single vehicle, is small enough to train and run cheaply, and whose behaviour under
attack is measured. That local detector is the focus of this work.

\textbf{This work.} We address G1--G5 with a single receiver-side detector and an evaluation protocol: neighbour attention measured as an
ablation and under growing shares of malicious neighbours (G1); precision, recall, F1, accuracy, false-positive rate and latency with significance
tests (G2); 14 attack types of VeReMi Extension (G3); plausibility checks and training with careful lies, plus a collusion stress test (G4); and
message-level labels next to vehicle-level ones (G5).

\section{Our Approach}
\label{sec:approach}
Fig.~\ref{fig:pipeline} shows the pipeline. Raw messages heard by a receiver are grouped into windows, each claim is checked for plausibility,
a transformer scores every sender, and a receiver-side memory smooths the scores over time before the alarm threshold is applied.

\subsection{Receiver-centric windows}
Each sample is one receiver window as defined in Section~\ref{sec:problem}. Per sender and step we keep the raw claim: position, speed, heading,
claimed acceleration along the heading, and the number of messages heard in that second. Sender identities and the dataset's simulated noise
fields are never inputs. Windows are skipped when the receiver's own state is missing in any of the 10 steps.

\subsection{Inputs: claims plus plausibility checks}
\label{sec:features}
For every sender and step the model receives 19 values. Nine describe the claim itself: position relative to the receiver (scaled by 300\,m),
speed, cosine and sine of the heading, position change since the previous step, a validity flag, and $\log(1+\text{messages per second})$, which
makes floods visible. Ten are plausibility checks computed from the claims:
\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{Kinematics}: for claimed position $p_t$, speed $v_t$, heading $\theta_t$ and acceleration $a_t$ we use $a_t$,
$(v_t-v_{t-1})-a_t$, $\lVert p_t-p_{t-1}\rVert - v_t$ and, when the sender moved more than 1\,m,
$1-\cos(\angle(p_t-p_{t-1})-\theta_t)$. Each is zero for a consistent honest vehicle up to sensor noise.
\item \emph{Road map}: from the receivers' own positions in the training hour we build a 5\,m grid of where vehicles actually drive (675$\times$667
cells) with the dominant driving direction per cell. Inputs are the on-road density at the claimed position and the alignment of the claimed heading
with the road. The map stands in for the digital map a vehicle carries; no test data are used to build it.
\item \emph{Neighbours}: distance from the claimed position to the nearest other sender's claimed position at the same step.
\item \emph{Exact repeats}: whether the claimed position equals (to 1\,cm) one of the sender's own earlier claims, and whether it equals a position
that another sender claimed earlier (a copied trail); plus the logarithm of the distance moved, so centimetre-level movement is visible. Real GPS
fixes always jitter: no honest step in 1.38 million had zero movement, while frozen and replayed claims repeat exactly.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Model}
A factorised transformer encodes the window: a linear embedding ($d{=}64$) with a learned time encoding, then two blocks, each with
(i) temporal self-attention of a sender over its own 10 steps, (ii) spatial self-attention across the senders present at the same step (the
neighbourhood), and (iii) a feed-forward layer; 4 heads, dropout 0.1, pre-normalisation. The representation of the newest step feeds a two-layer
classification head. Padding and missing steps are masked. The ablation without neighbour attention restricts the spatial attention to the
sender itself, so both variants have the same 144k parameters.

\subsection{Training with careful lies}
\label{sec:aug}
The benchmark's attacks are mostly physically implausible. To teach the model consistent lies as well, we add a copy of half of the training
windows (those with at least two honest senders) in which 1--4 honest senders are rewritten as liars, coordinated or independent: a position offset
of 20--300\,m that keeps the true motion, or a fake stop (position frozen with GPS-like jitter, speed zero, acceleration consistent with the
braking). A third lie type, a convoy driving at the receiver, is \emph{held out} of training to test generalisation. All checks are recomputed on
the rewritten claims. This adds 39,691 windows with about 74,000 liar slots.

\subsection{Receiver memory}
A receiver that has flagged a sender keeps a decaying suspicion of it: $s'_t=\max(s_t,\lambda s'_{t-1})$ over that receiver's windows of the same
sender, forgotten after 30\,s of silence. $\lambda$ and the alarm threshold are chosen on validation data.

\subsection{Message-level labels}
\label{sec:labels}
A received message is \emph{falsified} if its claimed position differs from the sender's true position at reception by more than 1\,m or its
speed by more than 0.5\,m/s, using the dataset's ground-truth trace. Honest claims match the ground truth exactly, so the tolerance only absorbs
rounding. An attacker slot is positive if the sender sent at least one falsified message in the window; DoS attackers, whose misbehaviour is the flood
itself, are always positive. Attacker slots without any falsified message are left out of message-level scoring. They are 2.0\% of all attacker
slots, almost all from EventualStop before its stop (26\% of its slots).

\subsection{Implementation}
Everything is written in Python with PyTorch, NumPy and scikit-learn. The VeReMi Extension logs (one JSON file per vehicle, Zenodo record 20090854,
CC-BY 4.0) are parsed into windows by a loader that reads message type 2 (the receiver's own state) and type 3 (received BSMs) and takes labels from
the file names; 250 receivers are sampled evenly per simulation slice. Parsing runs in parallel and is cached; the features of all windows are
written once to half-precision memory-mapped files, so training needs about 1\,GB of GPU memory and little RAM. Training uses weighted binary cross
entropy (positive weight 2), AdamW (learning rate $2{\times}10^{-3}$, weight decay $10^{-2}$), a one-cycle schedule, batch size 128 and 8 epochs,
about 10--15 minutes per model on a laptop GPU (RTX 3050 Ti). Inference takes well under a millisecond per window. The code, trained models, logs and the scripts
that produce every table are in the project repository; the main entry points are \texttt{run\_v2.py} (features and training),
\texttt{label\_active.py} (message labels), \texttt{run\_analysis\_v2.py} and \texttt{run\_collusion\_v2.py}. Table~\ref{tab:impl} summarises the
settings. All random choices (receiver sampling, augmentation, injected lies, bootstrap) use fixed seeds; rebuilding the test windows of our first model from
its saved weights on a second machine reproduced its reported numbers exactly.

\section{Evaluation}
\label{sec:eval}
\textbf{Protocol.} Models are trained on the first simulated hour of each of the 14 attack subsets (109,823 windows) and tested on the second hour,
whose receivers are split into contiguous validation (30\%, 29,547 windows) and test (70\%, 68,971 windows) blocks. Thresholds, memory decay and
rule thresholds are chosen on validation only. We report precision, recall, F1, accuracy and false-positive rate (FPR), each the mean of two
training seeds (the seeds differ by at most 0.5 points). Per-attack F1 scores an attack against the honest senders of its own simulation run.
Differences between models are tested with a paired bootstrap over receivers (2,000 resamples) and Holm correction across attacks. Baseline rules
are the range check and the speed-consistency check of VeReMi~\cite{vanderheijden2018}, applied to the same windows.

\textbf{Overall (Table~\ref{tab:v2}).} With message labels the detector reaches F1 97.88\% (recall 97.12\%); with receiver memory F1 is 97.97\%, and
precision, recall, F1 and accuracy are all above 97.4\% in both seeds. With vehicle labels F1 is 97.01\% (97.12\% with memory): recall falls because the honest-looking
windows of attackers count as misses. Our first model, with the same architecture but only the nine raw inputs, reached F1 94.9\% (precision 98.4\%,
recall 91.6\%, accuracy 96.9\%, vehicle labels); the plausibility checks raised ConstPosOffset recall from 0.66 to 0.97 and EventualStop recall
on active windows to 0.99. The speed-consistency rule reaches F1 80.0\% and fails completely on offset positions, EventualStop, delayed messages and
DoS (Table~\ref{tab:perattack}). Mean time to the first alarm is 0.9\,s (median 0, counted from the first window that contains the sender, which
already holds up to 10\,s of history); 96\% of attacker--receiver pairs are flagged at some point.

\textbf{Neighbour attention.} Attending across senders adds 0.3 F1 points (95\% bootstrap interval about [0.2, 0.4] in both seeds and both label
schemes), is significantly better on 8--10 of the 14 attacks and significantly worse on none, and lowers the FPR from 0.90\% to 0.60\%. Its benefit
depends on the neighbourhood (Table~\ref{tab:share}): within each attack type, the gain shrinks as the share of attacker vehicles in the window
grows and turns negative ($-0.004$) once attackers are the majority. In the earlier model without checks the same pattern was stronger (from
$+0.071$ to $-0.023$). Neighbour context helps while most neighbours are honest; it is not a defence against a malicious majority.

\textbf{Consistent and colluding lies (Table~\ref{tab:traj}).} We inject lies into the test windows, recompute all inputs, and compare models at
the same 1\% validation FPR. Before the checks, both our models caught at most 4\% of such colluders, coordinated or not; neighbour attention even
let coordinated convoys raise false alarms on honest neighbours from 0.9\% to 3.0\%. With the checks, a 50\,m offset is caught in 83--86\% of
cases (63\% at 25\,m, 98\% at 400\,m), a fake stop in about 70\%, and the held-out convoy in 87--90\%. Training with careful lies is what makes fake
stops detectable (1\% without it) and costs 0.2 F1 points on the benchmark attacks. Fig.~\ref{fig:offset} shows the effect of the
checks as a function of the size of the lie.

\textbf{Reference numbers.} The survey~\cite{hakeem2025} lists 97.37\% for VeReMi (accuracy, precision, recall and F1 reported together) and
F1 98\% for positional attacks with a reputation-based detector~\cite{gyawali2020}; So \emph{et al.} report precision 95.91\% and detection rate
83.73\% with physical-layer checks~\cite{so2019}. Our message-level results exceed 97.37\% on all four measures (position attacks alone: F1 97.5--99.4\%).
These figures come from a different dataset (original VeReMi, five attacks) and partly undocumented protocols; they place our results but are not a
like-for-like comparison.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}
\textbf{What the detector relies on.} The progression from our first model to the final one shows where the accuracy comes from. The first model
saw the same senders and the same neighbourhood but only the raw claims; it learned to flag claims that are physically impossible (positions beyond
radio range, speed that does not match movement) and missed everything else, including 34\% of ConstPosOffset attackers. The plausibility checks
turn knowledge a vehicle already has (a map, the laws of motion, the fact that GPS readings never repeat exactly) into explicit inputs, and the
largest gains come from exactly the attacks that are consistent in themselves: offsets inside the radio range, frozen positions and replayed
trails. Neighbour attention is a second-order effect on top: it lowers false alarms, probably because an honest vehicle that looks unusual (for
example, one stopping hard) is easier to accept when its neighbours behave the same way.

\textbf{Neighbours as evidence, and as a risk.} The attacker-share analysis and the stress test agree. Comparing a sender with its neighbours is
useful when the neighbours are mostly honest, and it becomes a liability when they are not: coordinated liars barely affect their own detection
but can make the model doubt honest vehicles around them. A deployed system should therefore not treat agreement among neighbours as proof,
and it should cap how much any group of senders can change the score of another sender.

\textbf{Deployment.} The model has 144k parameters and scores a window in well under a millisecond on a laptop GPU, so a single vehicle could
run it on every incoming window. Three parts need care in practice. The road map must come from map data rather than from recorded traffic; the
alarm threshold trades false alarms against missed attackers and should be set per deployment; and the receiver memory delays the release of a
falsely flagged sender by a few seconds, which is acceptable for misbehaviour reports but not for discarding safety-critical messages outright.

\textbf{An adaptive attacker.} An attacker who knows the checks will keep its lies on the road, smooth and consistent with its claimed speed and
acceleration, and will add jitter to frozen positions. Our map-aware stress test models this attacker, and detection drops to 23--48\%. Content
checks alone cannot close this gap: the receiver would need independent evidence of where the sender really is, such as signal strength or
angle of arrival, its own camera and radar, or reports from roadside units.

\section{Limitations and Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}
The benchmark is simulated; 14 of 19 attack types are covered (no Sybil attacks); results are the mean of two seeds; dense rush-hour traffic was
evaluated only for the earlier model; the road map assumes the receiver has a map of the area; and the collusion test rewrites honest vehicles of the
trace instead of simulating new attackers, without re-selecting the 16 senders after the lie. The message-level labels are a choice we make
explicit, and we report vehicle-level results next to them.

Within these limits, the study gives three answers. Plausibility checks computed from the claims (kinematics, a learned road map, exact repeats and
copied trails) did most of the work: they moved the detector from F1 94.9\% to about 98\% and turned consistent lies from invisible to mostly
detected. Neighbour attention gives a small, significant gain and fewer false alarms, but its benefit disappears once most neighbours are
malicious. A lie that stays on the road and obeys the physics cannot be separated from the truth using message content alone; closing that gap needs
an independent signal such as received signal strength, on-board sensors or roadside units, together with Sybil attacks and the newer VeReMi
NextGen data, which are our next steps.
